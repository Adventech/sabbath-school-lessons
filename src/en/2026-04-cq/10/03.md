### Organizational Authority

God uses prophets to organize His people and move them to action. He called Moses to lead the deliverance of Israel from Egypt and to help them become established as a new nation. Hosea 12:13 makes it clear that “by a prophet the Lord brought Israel out of Egypt, and by a prophet he was preserved.” Protecting and guiding God’s people was a formative and essential part of the biblical prophets’ work.

Prophets played a crucial role in revealing God’s leadership strategy for His people. In times of war and crisis, prophets were the means by which God offered direction, encouragement, and hope to His people and their leaders, as He did through Deborah and Jehaziel (Judg. 4:4–8; 2 Chron. 20:14–20). Prophets also gave guidance concerning festivals and worship (2 Chron. 29:25–29). At other times, God spoke to prophets concerning the governance of the nation (Deut. 1:9–16). In short, no area of Israelite life was beyond the direction and guidance of God through His chosen messengers.

In the New Testament, God used prophets to guide the establishment of the church. Imagine beginning a new organization without an operational manual and no visible leader. Such an organization is sure to experience serious challenges, if it survives at all. In a certain sense, this was the situation in the early Christian church. Jesus had ascended and was no longer with His disciples. They had a clear directive from Him—“Go into all the world and preach” (Mark 16:15), and while the Twelve Apostles had been chosen, there was no clear organizational structure to help them get started.

The early church faced many challenges, such as church organization, structure for mission, and the need to identify and correct heresy and immorality among them. In a real sense, the prophetic gift operating in the early church played a critical role in its preservation and protection (Acts 13:1, 2). The prophetic gift guided how the believers responded to people who needed to be separated from the church (1 Cor. 5:1–5). When certain groups of believers felt neglected and division threatened the church, God led them to create new positions and delegate certain responsibilities to provide better care for all (Acts 6:1–7). As the church expanded and new church plants were added, the prophetic gift encouraged strong organization in each congregation (Titus 1:5). It’s difficult to envision how the early church would’ve gotten started without the prophetic gift helping to organize it and move it forward for mission.
