**Read This Week’s Passage:** Genesis 3

### Communication in Eden

Digital technology allows many of us to see friends and relatives in faraway places—a great blessing, to be sure, but how much better to meet in person! Something special happens when we get together, exchange hugs, and talk face-to-face.

Human beings were created to enjoy face-to-face friendship with God and one another. Genesis 1 and 2 tell the Creation story. God created a beautiful world, and in it He put Adam and Eve, the crowning act of Creation. Humans differ from every other creature on earth because we were made “in the image of God” (Gen. 1:27), something said only about us and not, for example, the birds or animals. As such, we were to live wholistically abundant lives, experience love in all its forms, and walk in communion with God Himself.

We read in Genesis 2:18–23 that God created Eve for Adam because, God said, it was “not good that man should be alone” (Gen. 2:18). Right from the beginning, humans were created as social beings. Sociologists today teach that human interaction provides numerous benefits to our physical and mental well-being, while loneliness is a killer. It’s no wonder, then, that after the creation of Eve, Adam exclaimed, “This is now bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh; she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man” (Gen. 2:23). The familial structure established by one man and one woman joined in matrimony is the first social institution God provided for humans in order for them to experience wholistic relationship and communion. It should come as no surprise that one of Satan’s main goals has always been to destroy the fundamental dynamics of the family.

In their original home, Adam and Eve could meet with God and listen to His instructions. As stewards of God’s wonderful garden, they grew in knowledge and skill as they cared for the flora and fauna. Each day, they also had special time with God “in the cool of the day” (Gen. 3:8). The Sabbath day in particular, an essential part of God’s creative act, was a dedicated time of communion between God and His children (Gen. 2:2, 3; Exod. 20:8–11). On that day, our first parents experienced God’s love and care for them in a personal way.

Sin, however, disrupted the first family’s peace and damaged their intimate connection with God. Adam and Eve were expelled from the Garden and could no longer speak with God face-to-face. The prophet Isaiah wrote about this challenging human condition: “Your iniquities have separated you from your God; and your sins have hidden His face from you” (Isa. 59:2).

In truth, Adam and Eve were still God’s children. However, the results of sin could not be avoided, and they had to leave Eden. Nothing was the same after their disobedience. Sin entirely changed human life in ways we cannot imagine, and that’s because all we know is a world of sin. Because of sin, the land was “cursed” and would produce “thorns and thistles” (Gen. 3:17, 18). Humans would now experience pain, and they would have to “sweat” in order to provide food for themselves, only to die and return to the ground (Gen. 3:19). Because of sin, people lost face-to-face communication with God.

To restore that communication and relationship with His beloved creatures, God has provided ways to speak to us. His most recognized form of divine interaction with us is through carefully chosen spokespersons, known as prophets, who reveal His messages to us. The prophets we know of often have writings in the Bible, but not always.

God’s ultimate plan is to restore His image in us—the image that sin has shattered. This is what the plan of salvation is about: a restoration that will be complete when God creates “new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells” (2 Pet. 3:13). Until then, God still talks to us, and He still sometimes uses prophets to do so.
