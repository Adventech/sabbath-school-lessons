### The Cultural and Historical Context

The intended meaning of a biblical passage is not always immediately obvious to the reader. Sometimes a text may appear to say something that contradicts other parts of Scripture. Knowing the cultural and historical context of a passage can help explain the original idea behind it.

For example, taking Isaiah 65:20 out of its historical setting, one could easily assume that death and sinners will be in the new heavens and the new earth. However, that idea contradicts the promises in Isaiah that the righteous dead will be resurrected (Isa. 26:19) and that death and sorrow will finally cease to exist (Isa. 25:8; 65:19).

Isaiah 65:20 is, obviously, symbolic language. After all, what child is one hundred years old? Instead, when reading Isaiah 65:17–20 in its historical setting, we can recognize that the verse was poetically pointing to the blessing that the nation of Israel would receive, but only if they remained faithful to the covenant stipulations (cf. Deut. 28). As Jesus did in Matthew 24, linking more imminent events with later ones, Isaiah also did here, showing that what would happen in the prophet’s time would be a precursor to the new heavens and new earth.

In Isaiah, the new creation is not totally in the future. It has entered suddenly into the present through God’s glorious work of redemption and forgiveness. The verse announces that even now, in the present existence of Israel, God is already defeating death. This idea is expressed through the promise of longevity and the elimination of infant mortality (Isa. 65:20). All this only foreshadows what will eventually come.

Other texts of Scripture also challenge today’s readers to know how best to apply the text to our present culture and time. For example, 1 Corinthians 16:20 tells believers that they should “greet one another with a holy kiss.” In the Greek culture of New Testament times, a greeting among relatives and close friends was accompanied by a kiss. Paul also instructed that this warm, affectionate greeting be given among Christian believers in Romans 16:16, 2 Corinthians 13:12, and 1 Thessalonians 5:26. From the context, we can extract the biblical principle of greeting believers warmly and making them feel part of the family of God. In some cultures, kissing would be inappropriate, and a handshake, hug, or bow would be more culturally acceptable. Regardless of the cultural expression, however, Paul encouraged us to make people feel accepted and loved in church gatherings.
