### The Bible Interprets Itself

Our interpretation of Scripture is largely influenced by our assumptions. Those who consider the Bible as a mere collection of ancient documents are unable to see any unity among its various books. However, by taking into consideration the basic principle that “all Scripture is inspired by God and beneficial” (2 Tim. 3:16, NASB), we can appreciate its underlying unity and allow the Bible to be its own interpreter.

The New Testament increases our understanding of the Old Testament. For example, Christ’s Sermon on the Mount provides deep insight into the spiritual meaning and purpose of the Old Testament laws. A superficial reading of Matthew 5:17–48 can give the impression that Jesus was replacing the teachings of the Old Testament with new ones of His own. However, a more careful reading makes clear that Jesus was unfolding the deep, spiritual dimensions of the Old Testament. In this sense, He was fulfilling the promise of Jeremiah 31:31–34 that under the new covenant, God’s law would be put in the minds and written on the hearts of His people (cf. Heb. 8:8–10; 10:16).

Sometimes the interpretation of a given passage can be found within the same book. For example, in Daniel 2, we find the description of the mysterious dream of King Nebuchadnezzar (Dan. 2:31–35) followed by the interpretation of the dream (Dan. 2:36–45). Likewise, in Matthew 13 we have the parable of the sower (Matt. 13:3–9) and then its interpretation (Matt. 13:18–23).

Sometimes, a message’s interpretation is provided by a later prophet. For instance, 2 Chronicles 36:20–23 records the fulfillment of the prophecy of the seventy years, which was predicted in Jeremiah 25:11, 12 and Jeremiah 29:10. However, many Old Testament symbols were fulfilled in Christ, and many Old Testament passages are interpreted in the New Testament.

“The Old Testament is the gospel in figures and symbols. The New Testament is the substance. One is as essential as the other.”<sup>1</sup> Indeed, the New Testament is the inspired interpreter of the Old Testament.

Inspired by the Holy Spirit, some prophets recorded things that they themselves did not fully understand. Sometimes, later prophets understood the meaning of ancient prophecies better than those who first received the prophecies (1 Pet. 1:10–12). Discerning the same Spirit behind each biblical author leads us to interpret the Bible as a connected whole, not as a collection of disjointed messages. Each book of the Bible provides a unique contribution to a single narrative of God loving us and doing everything possible to save all who will cooperate with Him.

**Questions to consider:**

`Why is it crucial to be aware of our assumptions as we study the Bible? Why must our most important assumptions be that it truly is the Word of God and that it passes judgment on us, not us on it?`
