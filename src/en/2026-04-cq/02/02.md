### Isaiah, the Gospel Prophet

Isaiah was a major prophet of the Old Testament whose writings are often quoted or referenced in the New Testament. He received his call to prophetic ministry directly from God. Overwhelmed by God’s holiness and majesty, Isaiah realized his unworthiness. In that moment, a seraph flew to Isaiah with a live coal from the heavenly altar and “purged” his sins (Isa. 6:7). God then asked, “Whom shall I send, and who will go for Us?” Isaiah’s response should be ours: “Here am I! Send me” (Isa. 6:8).

God prepared Isaiah for disappointing responses to his ministry. Often, it would seem that nobody was listening to the messages God gave him (Isa. 6:9, 10). However, even when his work appeared unfruitful, Isaiah had to continue sharing God’s messages and trusting Him with the results.

Isaiah’s call came at a pivotal point in Israel’s history. Despite his own failings, the task of warning Israel and its leaders of their sins was very important. Isaiah’s prophecies are key Scripture passages, providing profound insight into God’s nature, humanity’s nature, and the unfolding plan of salvation. In fact, what is unquestionably one of the most powerful prophecies, depicting not only Jesus’ death but also what His death did for the world, is found in the famous chapter of Isaiah 53.

Isaiah’s prophecies foretold the Messiah’s purpose and mission (Isa. 53:3–5). They explained why Christ was “led like a lamb to the slaughter” (Isa. 53:7, NIV) and how His atoning sacrifice saved His people from their sins. The substitutionary aspect of Christ’s death is explicitly depicted, revealing the foundation of the atonement made on behalf of all humanity. “All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned, every one, to his own way; and the Lord has laid on Him the iniquity of us all” (Isa. 53:6). This one verse succinctly depicts the universal nature of sin as well as the universal nature of the Cross, the solution to sin. This chapter, perhaps more than any other in either the Old or New Testament, shows the plan of salvation in clear and unambiguous terms.

Isaiah’s prophetic calling was not easy. People found his dire predictions of their destruction preposterous, as many do today when warned about the consequences of sin (Rom. 6:23). Isaiah’s prophetic message for his time and ours is clear (see Isa. 2:4; 5:20). It was the nation’s refusal to reform and practice justice that led to Jerusalem’s destruction. The same sin will mark the destruction of earth’s wicked inhabitants when Christ returns.
