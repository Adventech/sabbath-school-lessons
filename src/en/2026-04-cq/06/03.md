### Literary Sources

As we examine the biblical prophets’ writings, we discover that in addition to using literary assistants, they sometimes drew on outside source materials. For example, Solomon indicated that he didn’t compose all the proverbs he recorded, portraying himself instead as a collector and compiler of various proverbs: “And moreover, because the Preacher was wise, he still taught the people knowledge; yes, he pondered and sought out and set in order many proverbs. The Preacher sought to find acceptable words; and what was written was upright—words of truth” (Eccles. 12:9, 10). Under the inspiration of God, he “sought out” many sayings and searched to find “acceptable words.” In fact, the oft-quoted description of the virtuous woman in Proverbs 31 is attributed to the mother of an otherwise unknown King Lemuel, not Solomon (Prov. 31:1). There’s some evidence that points to the influence of Egyptian literature on the Proverbs as well.

Similarly, the inspired historians of the Old Testament tell us they referenced archives and other historical annals to narrate faithfully the history of God’s people. To better understand the history of the kings, the writer of 1 Kings recommended reading certain other historical sources available at that time (1 Kings 11:41). Ezra and Nehemiah relied on historical records to document their accounts. The book of Ezra includes copies of letters and royal decrees regarding Jerusalem and Persia (see Ezra 4–7), and Nehemiah talked about genealogical registries that he found and incorporated into his narrative (see Neh. 7:5–72).

This principle appears in the New Testament as well. Luke is an example of an author who carefully researched and synthesized a variety of eyewitness reports while writing his gospel account. “It is clear from Luke’s experience that Inspiration functions in a manner consistent with the natural operation of the mental faculties and does not set them aside. Here is an inspired writer who was led by the Holy Spirit to give diligent study to the available oral and written source materials on the life of Christ, and then to combine into a connected narrative the information thus gathered.”<sup>1</sup>

Other New Testament books quote from non-biblical literature. For example, though he didn’t mention their names, Paul quoted from Greek philosophers (see Acts 17:28 and Titus 1:12). The book of Jude also seems to quote from 1 Enoch 1:9—a non-canonical, intertestamental Jewish work (Jude 14, 15).

The Holy Spirit, who inspired the prophets to write their messages, also guided the prophets to include certain sources that would help illustrate their messages, add clarity, and provide further documentation of sacred history.
