### Literary Assistants

When the princes in Jerusalem sat and listened to Baruch read the book containing the messages of Jeremiah the prophet, they immediately asked how the book had been written. Baruch explained that Jeremiah “proclaimed with his mouth all these words to me, and I wrote them with ink in the book” (Jer. 36:18). Perhaps Jeremiah could’ve written his own messages, but instead he relied on a trusted scribe to record his prophecies faithfully and to share them with the nation’s leaders.

Jeremiah is not the only person in the Bible to use the services of a literary assistant to communicate the divine message. In the New Testament book of Romans, even though Tertius said he “wrote this epistle” (Rom. 16:22), it’s evident from the beginning of the book that he was writing only what Paul wished to say. Tertius was his amanuensis, or secretary—a literary assistant who put Paul’s words into written form.

This practice was not unusual for Paul. At the conclusion of his letters to the Colossians and Thessalonians, Paul made a point of adding his salutation with “my own hand” (Col. 4:18; 2 Thess. 3:17) as a way of authenticating the entire letter. This suggests that he relied on someone else to pen the letters for him. Because this reality could’ve raised doubts as to whether the letter correctly represented him—or worse, whether it was from him at all—Paul provided his own handwritten note at the end so there would be an easy form of authentication.

Many commentators attribute the variation in literary style found among Paul’s letters to his use of literary assistants. The same is suspected regarding Peter’s two letters: “Although it is a fact that the style of language of 2 Peter is different from that of the first epistle, this may reasonably be accounted for by the probability that Peter . . . employed secretarial aid in phrasing an epistle he was writing in Greek. If Paul, who was fully at home in Greek, used secretaries, as he quite evidently did, it is even more logical to think that Peter, whose native language was Aramaic, would have done so, and that these secretaries would have exerted a definite influence on the wording of his letters in Greek.”<sup>1</sup> This suggests that while remaining faithful to the thoughts and meaning of God’s messengers, the vocabulary and sentence structure might reflect the varying literary abilities of the assistants they employed. Remember: it’s the thoughts that were inspired, not necessarily the specific words used to express those inspired thoughts.
