### Ellen G. White on Revelation and Inspiration

Seventh-day Adventists believe that Ellen G. White was divinely inspired. Her writings, life, and ministry meet the criteria of a true prophet. She possessed the same quality of inspiration as biblical prophets, though she never considered her writings to be the same as Scripture—and neither do we. On the contrary, Ellen White always uplifted the Bible. But how did she herself understand God’s prophetic revelations to her and the process of her inspiration?

First, Ellen White always considered the Bible as the authoritative and infallible revelation of God. The Bible reveals God’s truth and His plan of salvation for the fallen human race. Part of that plan, as we are learning, is using prophets to communicate His messages.

Second, Ellen recognized that the process of inspiration involved interaction between the divine and humans. God communicated His messages, but then the prophet conveyed the divine instruction using his or her own words. As Ellen put it, “It is not the words of the Bible that are inspired, but the men that were inspired. Inspiration acts not on the man’s words or his expressions but on the man himself, who, under the influence of the Holy Ghost, is imbued with thoughts. . . . The divine mind and will is combined with the human mind and will; thus the utterances of the man are the word of God.”<sup>1</sup>

Regarding visions and prophetic dreams, Ellen understood “thought inspiration” as a union of divine revelation and human experience. She wrote, “The Bible, with its God-given truths expressed in the language of men, presents a union of the divine and the human.”<sup>2</sup> God used many methods to effect an understanding of His message in the mind of His messenger. These included pictorial representations, words, and thoughts that engaged all the prophet’s senses. Ellen White’s experience was similar to that of Bible writers.

Third, Ellen noted that not all her human experiences and communications were inspired. “There are times when common things must be stated,” she advised, “common thoughts must occupy the mind, common letters must be written and information given that has passed from one to another of the workers. Such words, such information, are not given under the special inspiration of the Spirit of God.”<sup>3</sup> It’s vital to remember this distinction as we read and relate to her writings and experiences, particularly her correspondence and diaries.

Knowing Ellen White’s understanding of revelation and inspiration helps us avoid extremes and read her counsels prudently with understanding.
