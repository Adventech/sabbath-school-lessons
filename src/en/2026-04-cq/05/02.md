### Receiving and Writing the Messages

The apostle John was part of the inner circle of the Twelve Apostles (Luke 8:51; Matt. 17:1; Matt. 26:36, 37). After Christ’s ascension, John continued his ministry in Jerusalem. Before the city fell in a.d. 70, he moved to Ephesus to oversee churches in the Roman province of Asia Minor. During the Christian persecution under the Roman emperor Domitian, he was exiled to Patmos, an island in the Aegean Sea.

While there, John received special prophetic messages addressed not just to the seven churches of Asia Minor but to Christians throughout the ages (Rev. 1:1–3, 9–11). The prophetic message delivery chain, of which John was a part, looked something like this: God the Father, to Jesus Christ, to the angel, to the apostle John, and to the readers and hearers. This is a classic example of the revelation-inspiration process in which God uses prophets to communicate His messages to humanity.

If John hadn’t written down what was revealed to him, the message would have faded or become distorted with the passage of time. Jesus therefore instructed him to “Write the things which you have seen, and the things which are, and the things which will take place after this” (Rev. 1:19). Writing gives permanence to the spoken word, and that permanence allows the message to be preserved and transmitted through time.

Beginning with Moses, who authored the first five books of the Bible, the Holy Spirit guided the canonical prophets to produce the writings that now comprise the sixty-six books of the Bible. Shortly after the Israelites left Egypt, when they defeated the Amalekites, God instructed Moses to “write this for a memorial in the book” (Exod. 17:14), which provides a small demonstration of how intimately involved God was in the writing of His Scriptures.

The various Bible books reflect the literary styles and skills of their respective writers. The personal nuances and various styles of each author do not detract from their messages, because there are no “degrees” of inspiration. Some parts of the Bible are more obviously relevant and applicable to our lives and faith today than others, but that does not make one part of the Bible more inspired than another.

Sometimes Bible writers recorded inspired messages that were first delivered orally, either by themselves or by a trusted associate. In some instances, the prophets quoted non-inspired sources (Acts 17:28; 1 Cor. 15:33; Titus 1:12) and even the words of Satan (Gen. 3:1–5; Matt. 4:1–11). Furthermore, the Bible describes many good examples to be imitated but also many bad ones to be avoided (1 Cor. 10:1–11).

While reading the Bible, we should keep in mind that while it’s not written in perfect language, it carries an infallible message that will stand the test of time.
