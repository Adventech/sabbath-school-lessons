### Symbols, Parables, and Drama

The book of Revelation is filled with cryptic symbolism featuring various beasts and horns (Rev. 13), stars and candlesticks (Rev. 1), and symbolic women (Rev. 12 and 17), among other figures. Revelation follows a style similar to that of the Old Testament book of Daniel, in which the kingdoms and powers in the world were represented by the different parts of a great image (Dan. 2:31–45), various animals and horns (Daniel 7 and 8), and two kings (Daniel 11). Prophetic symbols like these can be overwhelming and intimidating to readers. Symbolic messages are not easily understood by the superficial reader, but diligent students slowly discover that the Bible provides its own interpretation.

Daniel and Revelation are not the only books of the Bible that speak symbolically and dramatically; the Bible contains a rich variety of word pictures and metaphors. For example, the Old Testament system of sacrifices, the Mosaic tabernacle, and the Temple in Jerusalem was a direct representation of Christ’s atoning sacrifice on the cross and His heavenly priesthood (Heb. 8:1–6).

Jesus’ teachings were full of metaphors. He spoke of Himself as the Bread of Life (John 6:35, 41–58), the Light of the world (John 8:12; 9:5), and the True Vine (John 15:1–8). Through the parable of the sower (Matt. 13:1–9, 18–23) and several others (Matt. 13:24–52), Jesus illustrated the nature and growth of His kingdom.

Another example comes via the vivid dramatization in Ezekiel 4:1–8, where God asked the prophet to make a miniature model of Jerusalem and to lay himself down, first on his left side for 390 days, representing the house of Israel, and then on his right side for 40 days, representing the house of Judah. Another visual experience was Hosea’s divinely ordained marriage to Gomer, a prostitute, representing Israel’s spiritual unfaithfulness to God (Hos. 1:2–9).

Ezekiel’s and Hosea’s dramatic experiences seem quite shocking today, but that was the point; they were supposed to be shocking, not just to us but also to the people back then. At a time when the warnings of the prophets were openly rejected (see 2 Chron. 36:15–21), the last act of divine mercy was to dramatically alert the people to their true condition in order to lead them to genuine repentance. Such a dramatic presentation certainly should’ve shown them how seriously they should’ve taken God’s warnings and pleas.
