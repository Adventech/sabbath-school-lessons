### Lighting the Flame

At the end of the 1800s, the Seventh-day Adventist Church suffered hard times. Many of the church’s first Bible workers died. Members fought with each other about Bible truths. Buildings burned down. At the same time, new leaders started to serve. One of these leaders was Elmer Ellsworth Andross.

Andross was born in 1868, in Minnesota. Andross wanted to work for the government. He wanted to make a lot of money and be famous, too. So, he chose to become a lawyer. But then his plans changed. He became a Bible worker. He accepted an invitation in 1899 to share the Seventh-day Adventist message in England. The Foreign Mission Group couldn’t promise to support Andross. But Andross and his wife, Sophie, decided to trust God. The Androsses arrived in Liverpool, England, full of hope.

In Liverpool, Andross helped start a new church of about 40 members. In the spring of 1900, Andross preached in the Midlands. Soon after, he preached in Birmingham. At the end of July, the church had 16 new Sabbath keepers. More people were interested in joining the church. In October, Andross was filled with joy. He said, “About 30 people promised to keep the Sabbath. . . . I never saw people who enjoyed Bible truth more! For sure, God’s angels went before us and touched people’s hearts. The angels got them ready to accept our message.”

In 1902, Andross became the president of a group of ­churches in North England. Under Andross’s leadership, the members in North England nearly doubled. The number of churches grew from six to 17.

In 1905, Andross became the president of the British Union. As president, Andross helped the church save a lot of money. Andross announced that soon the church wouldn’t need money from the General Conference anymore. Andross started the Stanborough Park headquarters in 1907. He also helped start the church’s work in printing, education, and health work. Andross was president of the British Union for three years. During this time, church membership grew 42%. The number of churches also grew, from 31 to 52 churches.

In 1908, sickness forced Andross and his family to go back to the United States. But Andross’s work in England continued to grow. Elmer E. Andross was more than a church leader. He was a Bible worker with a pastor’s heart. His years in England show how one man’s faith can change history. Because of Andross, the Seventh-day Adventist message became the same as a bright flame. This flame continues to shine to this day.
