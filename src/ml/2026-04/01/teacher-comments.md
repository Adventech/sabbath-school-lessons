#### Part I: Overview

Part I: Overview

Key Text: Hebrews 1:1, 2

Study Focus: Gen. 1:27, 28; Gen. 3:1–8, 14–24; Luke 15:11–32; Exod. 19:16–22; John 10:27–30.

**Introduction**

Introduction

In Genesis 2:4–25, Adam and Eve enjoyed intimate fellowship with God, walking and talking with Him face-to-face. However, after Adam and Eve’s tragic fall into sin disrupted this communion, humanity’s origi­nal parents lost their direct line of communication with the Creator. Yet, God did not abandon His pursuit of humanity. Instead, He established new ways to communicate, mediating His presence through prophets, the Written Word, and, ultimately, His Son, Jesus Christ. This first lesson affirms that, although God’s mode of communication changed after the Fall, His desire to speak with humanity has never ceased. Today, God continues communicating through the Scriptures, where the prophets and apostles bear unified witness to Christ. As a contemporary manifestation of this divine communication, Ellen G. White also points to Christ by affirming the biblical witness.

#### Part II: Commentary

Part II: Commentary

1. Adam and Eve enjoyed perfect fellowship with one another and intimate, face-to-face communion with God in the Garden of Eden.

Adam and Eve were created in a perfect world beyond our fondest imaginations. The Garden of Eden was the sacred dwelling place of God’s presence on earth. As the triune God is inherently relational, He created human beings in His image in order to reflect this relational nature. His greatest gift to unfallen humanity was the capacity to engage with Him in personal and relational communion. Adam and Eve thus enjoyed direct, face-to-face fellowship with God in this perfect setting.

2. Sin disrupted the perfect situation in the Garden of Eden, and humans lost face-to-face communion with God.

The moment that the sinless Adam and Eve partook of the forbidden fruit, they experienced a new sense of self-awareness: “Then the eyes of both were opened, and they knew that they were naked.” Consequently, “they sewed fig leaves together and made themselves loincloths” (Gen. 3:7, ESV). The intimate, perfect, face-to-face communion with God was broken. When the fearful pair “heard the sound of the Lord God walking in the garden in the cool of the day,” an experience they had previously joyfully welcomed, they now “hid themselves from the presence of the Lord God among the trees of the Garden” (Gen. 3:8, ESV).

According to Richard Davidson: “When God comes to the garden after Adam and Eve have sinned, [H]e initiates an encounter that constitutes nothing less than a ‘legal process,’ a ‘trial punishment by God.’ God begins the legal proceedings with an interrogation of the ‘defendants,’ and the defensive and accusatory responses by Adam and Eve (Gen. 3:9–14) indicate the rupture in interhuman (husband-wife) and divine-human relationships that has occurred as a result of sin. Following the legal interrogation and establishment of guilt, God pronounces the sentence in the form of curses (over the serpent and the ground, vv. 14, 17) and judgments (for the man and the woman, vv. 16–19).”—Davidson, Flame of Yahweh: Sexuality in the Old Testament (Peabody, MA: Hendrickson Publishers, 2007), pp. 58, 59.

Sad to say, Adam and Eve were cast out of the Garden, and God “placed the cherubim and a flaming sword” to guard the “way to the tree of life” (Gen. 3:24, ESV). “The cherubim guarding Eden represent more than armed guards. They signal the beginning of God’s presence drawn back to the heavens, and a mediated presence on earth through symbols and messengers/angels.”—Dru Johnson, The Universal Story: Genesis 1–11,  Transformative Word, ed. Craig G. Bartholomew et al. (Bellingham, WA: Lexham Press, 2018), p. 52.

3. As soon as sin entered the world, God took the initiative to restore His relationship with lost humanity.

Adam and Eve were devastated. Their world had changed for the worse in an instant. As the guilty pair listened to God pronounce curses and judgments, they heard a promise of hope. At the very heart of these judgments, the gospel of grace appeared. A promised Seed would come and defeat the serpent and accomplish salvation for human beings. Genesis 3:15 is the literary center of Genesis 3 and is considered the first announcement of the gospel.

Immediately after Adam and Eve’s fall into sin, God announced that all future divine revelations would center on the person and mission of the coming Redeemer. The rest of Scripture flows out of this promise. “This astounding proclamation of God’s determination to send the Seed and defeat the serpent (Satan) in order to secure humanity’s redemption is the foundation and key for the recognition of the prophetic voice in the Old Testament.”—Jiřί Moskala, “The Prophetic Voice in the Old Testament: An Overview,” in Alberto R. Timm and Dwain N. Esmond, eds., The Gift of Prophecy in Scripture and History (Silver Spring, MD: Review and Herald Publishing Association, 2015), p. 11.

Interestingly, the word used for “called” in Genesis 3:9 is the same word used when God called the prophets to their ministry (Isa. 51:2). Although it is used here in the context of judgment, the connotation of connection and relationship is present.

4. After the Fall, by speaking in various ways, ultimately in the person of Jesus Christ, God opened the lines of communication with sinful humanity.

No longer able to see God with mortal sight or hear His voice audibly, human beings received divine communication through human intermediaries. Hebrews 1:1 says that God “spoke to our fathers by the prophets” (ESV). For centuries, God communicated with His people through such spokespersons as Noah, Enoch, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, Samuel, David, and the canonical prophets. He gave revelations to these individuals “at many times and in many ways,” such as theophanies, dreams and visions, divine speech, and angels. He also spoke through the Urim and Thummim, miracles, and signs. These are the ways in which God spoke in the past during the Old Testament era.

“But in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son” (Heb. 1:2, ESV). The words of the Old Testament are still authoritative as the Word of God, but they must be read through the fulfillment realized in Jesus Christ. He is the ultimate and definitive revelation of God. “The old came in fragmentary, episodic fashion—here a little, there a little—and, as a result, was incomplete, partial, and imperfect. But the new comes in one climactic speech of God the Father, at one time, in one person: His Son. With Jesus, all the revelation of God from the OT is gathered up and ­surpassed.”—William G. Johnsson, “Hebrews,” in Ángel M. Rodriguez, ed., Andrews Bible Commentary (Berrien Springs, MI: Andrews University Press, 2022), pp. 1824, 1825.

5. Ellen G. White’s conversion experience prepared her to be a Christ-centered, contemporary manifestation of the prophetic gift.

The prophetic gift beyond biblical times will not add anything to the New Testament but, rather, will affirm its attestation to Christ. Young Ellen White’s conversion story laid the foundation for a lifelong love and passion for Jesus Christ. She viewed the heavenly Father as a God of love who wanted to save people through the redemptive works of His Son. This perspective would become the keynote of her prophetic ministry and her favorite theme: the paternal love of God.

In one of her most moving statements, Ellen White wrote: “All the paternal love which has come down from generation to generation through the channel of human hearts, all the springs of tenderness which have opened in the souls of men, are but as a tiny rill to the boundless ocean when compared with the infinite, exhaustless love of God. Tongue cannot utter it; pen cannot portray it. You may meditate upon it every day of your life; you may search the Scriptures diligently in order to understand it; you may summon every power and capability that God has given you, in the endeavor to comprehend the love and compassion of the heavenly Father; and yet there is an infinity beyond. You may study that love for ages; yet you can never fully comprehend the length and the breadth, the depth and the height, of the love of God in giving His Son to die for the world. Eternity itself can never fully reveal it. Yet as we study the Bible and meditate upon the life of Christ and the plan of redemption, these great themes will open to our understanding more and more.”—Testimonies for the Church, vol. 5, p. 740.

#### Part III: Life Application

Part III: Life Application

The following life application questions, drawn from the above commentary, are offered to enrich your class discussion. Choose from among them, and from those in the lesson, the ones that will best engage your class in meaningful reflection.

1.	Before sin, Adam and Eve spoke with God “with no obscuring veil between.”—Ellen G. White, Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 50. What do you imagine it was like to experience face-to-face fellowship with the Almighty in the Garden of Eden, “with no obscuring veil between”?

2.	The Hebrew word for “know” means relational and experiential knowledge, as well as intellectual knowing and understanding. In light of this definition, how did Adam and Eve “know” they were “naked” (Gen. 3:7)?

3.	How did sin change the communication between God and human beings?

4.	What does living in God’s presence mean for us today, even though we no longer see Him face-to-face?

5.	How can we cultivate a deeper personal relationship with God daily, as Adam and Eve once experienced in Eden?

6.	What does it mean that all Scripture flows from the first gospel promise in Genesis 3:15? How do we see this theme repeated throughout the Bible?

7.	Hebrews 1:1, 2 says that, in the past, God spoke through prophets, but in the last days, He has spoken through His Son. How do Jesus’ life and teachings give us the clearest picture of God?

8.	In what ways does God still communicate with us today? How can we become more attentive to His voice?

9.	Ellen G. White’s favorite theme was the paternal love of God. How does reflecting on God’s love change our perspective on life and faith?
